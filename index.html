Two BLDC motors for an electric boat, designed and built from scratch

I designed and simulated two custom brushless DC inrunner motors for an electric boat, then fabricated, wound, and assembled them. They drive the propellers through a timing belt and pulley system, and our team raced the boat in a competitive boat race.

January to May 2026Team of four engineersANSYS, CAD, laser cutting, machining
Type
BLDC inrunner
Slots and poles
12 slots, 10 poles
Magnets
N52 permanent magnets
Units built
2
Winding
2 layers, 17 conductors per slot
Wire
2 strands of 0.724 mm copper
Core
Laser-cut laminations, varnish insulated
Drive
Timing belt and pulleys
  1. 1

    Simulate the electromagnetics

    Before cutting any metal, I modeled both motors in ANSYS to study their electromagnetic behavior, magnetic flux distribution, torque, and efficiency. The winding layout uses two layers on 12 slots, with 17 conductors per slot.

  2. 2

    Model every part in CAD

    I built 3D CAD models of the motor components and mechanical assemblies to set the dimensions, check that every part fit, and prepare the designs for manufacturing.

  3. 3

    Laser-cut the laminations

    The stator core is a stack of thin laser-cut laminations instead of one solid piece of metal. I used laser cutting and machining to make the motor components.

  4. 4

    Insulate every layer

    Each lamination got a coat of red insulating varnish so the layers are electrically isolated from one another. That reduces eddy currents and the energy they waste.

  5. 5

    Stack and wind the stator

    The varnished laminations were stacked into the stator core and wound with custom copper windings for the 12-slot, 10-pole layout. The rotor carries N52 permanent magnets.

  6. 6

    Assemble, drive, and race

    I assembled the motors and integrated them with a timing belt and pulley drive to complete the boat's propulsion system. We tested it and used it in a competitive boat race.

A 741 op-amp built from 20 discrete transistors

For ECE 2225 (Lab 6, October 2026), I laid out the internal circuit of a 741 operational amplifier in KiCad, then populated and hand-soldered the PCB with 20 BJTs, 11 resistors, and a 33 pF compensation capacitor. The board follows the standard 8-pin 741 pinout, so it works in any conventional op-amp test circuit.

I tested it on a breadboard as an inverting amplifier using a function generator, DC power supply, and oscilloscope. An earlier practice board (Lab 5) built up my KiCad layout, soldering, and assembly skills first.
